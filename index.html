Can AI win the $1M Netflix Challenge

Suppose you gave Alien five stars. How many stars would you give Aliens? Netflix posed that problem millions of times. It supplied viewers’ past ratings and kept some answers hidden. In 2006 it offered $1 million to anyone who could cut the error on those missing stars by 10% compared with its own system, Cinematch. Rival teams BellKor, BigChaos, and Pragmatic Theory joined forces as BellKor’s Pragmatic Chaos. After nearly three years and a close finish against The Ensemble, their July 2009 submission won the prize. Read Netflix’s announcement.

My experiment: Can AI match what the winning team achieved? I'm revisiting the movie-rating challenge with AI. At first, Codex's rapid progress made me think the challenge would be beaten quickly. When progress slowed, I brought Claude into the mix and had them build on each other's results.

Why? The Netflix Challenge has a special place in my heart. I have fond memories participating in the competition and following its progress. I still come back to it from time to time, long after the competition ended. At the time, it compelled me to learn how to build my own computer because my 512 MB XPS could not handle the amount of data. Even though the majority of my time was spent learning memory management in C and waiting days for computation results, I was super happy when my submissions scored 1.02 and 0.97. Neither beat Cinematch, but they were in the same ballpark. The idea of finally reaching the winning mark on my own machine makes this return especially rewarding.

01 / Calendar time

Overall progress · September–November

How did the guesses get better?

If we guess four stars and the viewer gave three, we missed by one star. Our score, root mean squared error (RMSE), gives extra weight to big misses. Lower is better. Netflix’s system scored 0.9525. To match a 10% reduction we needed 0.85725 on each of two groups of ratings recovered from the original contest.

  1. September · Learn individual taste

    Our first model learned how generous each viewer was and how popular each movie was. It scored 0.98257. That approach can miss a generous reviewer who hates horror. We combined models that learned which viewers tended to like the same movies. The first blend scored 0.89369 on the practice ratings.

  2. October · Learn from the movies people chose

    Someone rating three science-fiction films in a week tells us something even if we hide the stars. Using the other films each viewer chose around the prediction date lowered error from 0.866705 to 0.866485 on the same questions. All five groups of viewers improved. The gain fell below our 0.000300 cutoff, so the chart initially missed it.

  3. November 18 · Combine different clues

    “What did people with your tastes think?” and “How did you rate similar movies?” can give different answers. We added 19 freshly trained predictors to a blend that learned how much to trust each guess. On a separate practice set, error fell from 0.863658 to 0.862194. Every viewer group improved.

  4. November 24–27 · Put the ratings in time order

    The harder final group stood at 0.860599 on November 24. Learning when to trust each model from a viewer’s rating history helped close the gap. The last step gave different weights to the stars a viewer gave similar movies before and after the missing rating. That moved 0.85725978 to 0.85724395, crossing the line by 0.00000605. The other group finished at 0.85614526.

Was the October plateau just a strict scoring rule?

The rule hid some progress. The October movie-choice experiment beat its comparison in all five viewer groups and was recognized retroactively on November 18. Other ideas had little effect. A November 8 test tried 25 changes to the existing collection of predictors. Its best gain was only 0.000020, and two viewer groups did worse. The November 18 comparison kept the scoring rule the same on both sides, so its gain came from the added predictions.

There was one real payoff to keeping a model that looked past its best. After 23 passes through the training ratings, it scored 0.894582 on its own. After 32 passes, it scored a worse 0.895234. But the two versions disagreed about which ratings to raise or lower. Using a little of that difference improved the harder final group from 0.85727759 to 0.85727367. That experiment rewarded saving and comparing versions. It gave us no reason to expect that simply waiting longer would win.

03 / The playbook

What actually moved the needle

The development strategies below improved Probe scores. Each strategy records its method, result and limitation.

Foundation · Sep 27

Biases → time → factors

Regularized user/movie biases reached 0.98257. Temporal and frequency effects took the single-model score to 0.93492; 32-factor SVD++ reached 0.90267. Joint factor training later became the stronger backbone.

Lesson: time and interaction both matter.
Local structure · Sep 27

Neighbors break the factor ceiling

Learned item neighborhoods and residual kNN added movie relationships the shared latent space missed. B4 fell to 0.88666; expanding to 128 neighbors gave B7 0.88585.

Lesson: standalone rank is not ensemble value.
Ensemble · Sep 27–28

Mix banks, then correct errors

LightGBM combined factor, neighbor, RBM and AutoRec columns at 0.88040. A fixed cross-branch average reached 0.87822. Residual trees, wider kNN banks and metadata then drove R5-meta to 0.87481.

Lesson: diversity plus controlled blending pays.
Generative signal · Sep 28

Conditional RBMs

Six conditional RBM columns with differing seeds and hidden sizes moved the three-seed blend to 0.87312. Later near-duplicate columns brought tiny or inconsistent gains.

Lesson: architecture diversity beat another epoch.
Long histories · Sep 30–Oct 9

Masked sequence models

MH3 and MT variants crossed 0.871. Bayesian factors and longer masked histories helped in Round 8. A2h's 1536-token context with LightGBM + NN2 reached 0.86710 across all five folds.

Lesson: user history adds signal beyond scalar metadata.
Latent features · Oct 10

Expose latent features to the blender

R9 gave the tree and neural blender 22 frozen K1 latent and bias features on the existing 79-column bank. The fixed average reached 0.8668706, with every fold improved.

Lesson: better features can beat more raw predictors.
Fresh reproduction · November

Train the predictors and ensembles

Original factor, neighborhood, RBM, attention and CFNADE producers feed locally fitted tree and neural ensembles. Full model budgets, source hashes and saved-model replays keep each prediction traceable. Known user folds select candidates before qualifying predictions are frozen and audited.

Lesson: complete diverse model families, then measure their contribution.

Parallel history, GP and session work

Quan-only commits recorded rating-history pilots on Sep 28, a visible-context RBM pilot on Sep 30, then compact GP and session-context blends. The branch moved from C5 0.8759028 to C12 0.8747781 development CV. C12 narrowly beat historical R5 overall; the main Round 7 bank had already moved further. History pilots · session work.

Promoting an overall gain

R10-B5's item-frequency Bayesian column reached 0.8668212, but two folds worsened. The revised overall-score policy promotes its gain against the matched control; the original rejection remains in the history.

Interesting, weaker alone

The BAR paper port scored 0.9741851 Probe RMSE in its tested configuration. It improved its own baseline but did not challenge the ensemble.

Still open

Round 10 extracted kNN neighbor features for the blenders. The recorded branch launched a follow-up chain; no completed blend score was in the merged record.

Ten research branches, one main

The October 11 Codex work unified the parallel experiments without erasing the branch histories. That operation preserved the score trail and made the methods easier to inspect; it did not run a new scoring experiment.

Keep every branch
