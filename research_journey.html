Can AI win the $1M Netflix Challenge

Twenty years ago, in October 2006, Netflix offered $1 million to cut Cinematch’s 0.9525 Test RMSE by 10%, to 0.85725. Rival teams BellKor, BigChaos, and Pragmatic Theory joined forces as BellKor’s Pragmatic Chaos. After nearly three years and a close finish against The Ensemble, their July 2009 submission won the prize. Read Netflix’s announcement.

My experiment: Can AI match what the winning team achieved? I'm revisiting the movie-rating challenge with AI. At first, Codex's rapid progress made me think the challenge would be beaten quickly. When progress slowed, I brought Claude into the mix and had them build on each other's results.

Earlier public-bank result: expanding a public prediction bank and learning how to combine it. The final model reused hundreds of predictions released by gtakacs/netflix_prize, including pretrained ensemble outputs, alongside local model predictions. That integration produced the target-crossing recovered-label result; the hundreds of public models were already trained.

Current goal: reproduce the result with our own trained predictions. Our best verified development RMSE is 0.8593662, using published source code and no downloaded pretrained predictions. Our best standalone own107+50 context NN qualifying candidate scores 0.8609266 / 0.8619142 on the two recovered groups, so this fresh-training effort has not yet achieved the 10% target. See current progress and evidence.

Why? The Netflix Challenge has a special place in my heart. I have fond memories participating in the competition and following its progress. I still come back to it from time to time, long after the competition ended. At the time, it compelled me to learn how to build my own computer because my 512 MB XPS could not handle the amount of data. Even though the majority of my time was spent learning memory management in C and waiting days for computation results, I was super happy when my submissions scored 1.02 and 0.97. Neither beat Cinematch, but they were in the same ballpark. The idea of finally reaching the winning mark on my own machine makes this return especially rewarding.

Latest / November 15 Pacific · November 16 UTC

The 10% target reached

The expanded public prediction bank was the main breakthrough. Combining individual model predictions with pretrained ensemble outputs and local predictions, then fitting a contextual ridge blend, crossed 0.85725 on both recovered groups.

What is a public prediction bank?

A stored table of rating predictions: each row is a user–movie pair, and each column is one model’s prediction. “Public” means those predictions were released by other researchers. Our final bank combines 536 public individual predictors + 77 public pretrained ensemble outputs + 6 local inputs = 619 prediction columns. A regularized linear blender learns how to weight those columns, with weights that can vary by user, movie and date context.

The decisive integration: retain the individual predictions alongside the ensemble outputs. The public bank already existed before the target was reached; expanding its contents and changing the blend delivered the final gain. This result builds on substantial external pretraining. 03 / The playbook

What actually moved the needle

The development strategies below improved Probe scores. The November 15 public-bank integration then crossed the numerical target on recovered qualifying labels, a separate evaluation. Each strategy records its method, result and limitation.

Foundation · Sep 27

Biases → time → factors

Regularized user/movie biases reached 0.98257. Temporal and frequency effects took the single-model score to 0.93492; 32-factor SVD++ reached 0.90267. Joint factor training later became the stronger backbone.

Lesson: time and interaction both matter.
Local structure · Sep 27

Neighbors break the factor ceiling

Learned item neighborhoods and residual kNN added movie relationships the shared latent space missed. B4 fell to 0.88666; expanding to 128 neighbors gave B7 0.88585.

Lesson: standalone rank is not ensemble value.
Ensemble · Sep 27–28

Mix banks, then correct errors

LightGBM combined factor, neighbor, RBM and AutoRec columns at 0.88040. A fixed cross-branch average reached 0.87822. Residual trees, wider kNN banks and metadata then drove R5-meta to 0.87481.

Lesson: diversity plus controlled blending pays.
Generative signal · Sep 28

Conditional RBMs

Six conditional RBM columns with differing seeds and hidden sizes moved the three-seed blend to 0.87312. Later near-duplicate columns brought tiny or inconsistent gains.

Lesson: architecture diversity beat another epoch.
Long histories · Sep 30–Oct 9

Masked sequence models

MH3 and MT variants crossed 0.871. Bayesian factors and longer masked histories helped in Round 8. A2h's 1536-token context with LightGBM + NN2 reached 0.86710 across all five folds.

Lesson: user history adds signal beyond scalar metadata.
Latent features · Oct 10

Expose latent features to the blender

R9 gave the tree and neural blender 22 frozen K1 latent and bias features on the existing 79-column bank. The fixed average reached 0.8668706, with every fold improved.

Lesson: better features can beat more raw predictors.
Public bank breakthrough · Nov 15

Expand the public prediction bank

Reused 536 public individual predictors and 77 pretrained ensemble outputs from gtakacs/netflix_prize, plus six local inputs. A contextual ridge combined all 619 prediction columns. On the same recovered qualifying group, expanding the 83-input ridge to 619 inputs reduced RMSE from 0.8575942 to 0.8571903, crossing the 0.85725 target.

Lesson: integrating released predictions delivered the final gain.
